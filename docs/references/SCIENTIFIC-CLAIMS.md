# Scientific Claim Types

Classify claims so the evidence standard matches what is being asserted.

| Claim type | What it asserts | Typical evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | A property, pattern, or observation exists. | Direct observation, measurement, dataset, or source report. |
| Comparative | One method or condition differs from another. | Fair comparison, appropriate baselines, controlled conditions. |
| Causal | X produces or changes Y. | Identification strategy, intervention, controls, or strong causal argument. |
| Mechanistic | A particular process explains the outcome. | Predictions that distinguish the mechanism from alternatives; intermediate evidence where possible. |
| Theoretical | A formal proposition follows under stated assumptions. | Valid proof, derivation, or formal verification. |
| Generalization | A finding holds beyond the tested setting. | Evidence across relevant settings, populations, tasks, or distributions. |
| Novelty | The contribution is materially distinct from prior work. | Closest-work comparison supported by primary sources. |
| Practical | The method is useful under real constraints. | Realistic settings, cost/latency/reliability evidence, and operational constraints. |

## Research stage and contribution type

At the idea stage, develop a worthwhile problem, a concrete method, and a rationale connecting its design to the difficulty. Explain the expected contribution and compare known approaches. Use the table above to distinguish claims from established results and, when validation begins, to plan evidence collection. A detailed test plan or existing experimental support is not a prerequisite for proposing an empirical idea.

For empirical methods, improvements in capability, performance, efficiency, or reliability can carry the contribution. Develop formal theory when the work makes a theoretical claim, and competing mechanism explanations when the work claims to explain a process. Distinguish a design intuition from a demonstrated explanation.

Write directly about the proposed contribution and next action. Because proposals, hypothesis cards, and worked examples are already framed as unverified designs, state how the method operates without sentence-by-sentence reminders that the example is constructed or that experiments have not yet been run—simply never fabricate observed results or citations. Include uncertainty when it changes the recommendation, interpretation, or test design; state the concrete issue and how to resolve it. Omit routine disclaimers and repeated self-qualification.

## Claim strength

State the result and the conditions actually evaluated:
- "is observed in setting S" is narrower than "typically occurs";
- "is consistent with mechanism M" is weaker than "is caused by M";
- "outperforms baseline B on benchmark D" is narrower than "is better";
- "we did not find prior work in search boundary S" is narrower than "this is the first".

## Claim-evidence map

For an established result, link the core contribution claim to:
- one or more evidence records;
- the exact source or result locator;
- scope and assumptions;
- alternative explanations or limitations that affect interpretation, when relevant.

A claim with no evidence should be marked as a hypothesis or future-work proposition, not a result.
