# Hypothesis Development

Develop the strongest useful version of an idea before judging it. Read `docs/references/SCIENTIFIC-CLAIMS.md` for the evidence standard appropriate to its contribution type.

## Process

1. **State the problem.** Identify the task, setting, and limitation or opportunity that makes the idea worth trying.
2. **Make the proposal concrete.** Describe the method or intervention and how it changes the current approach. Component combinations, training recipes, losses, and prompt methods are valid candidates.
3. **Explain the intuition.** Give the practical reason the proposal could help. Use observations, analogies, or design reasoning; a complete mechanism or formal derivation is optional for empirical ideas.
4. **State the expected outcome.** Name what should improve or what finding should emerge, under which conditions, and how it can be measured.
5. **Sketch a first test.** Identify a useful comparison and the smallest experiment or analysis that would guide further investment.
6. **Name the contribution.** Explain the capability, efficiency, method, measurement, or understanding that success would add. Keep proposed outcomes distinct from observed results.

For a mechanistic or causal contribution, develop competing explanations and a comparison that distinguishes them. For a theoretical contribution, state the proposition, assumptions, and proof approach. Explore boundaries when they affect the proposed test or contribution.

## Output

Use `templates/hypothesis-card.md`. Include the problem, proposal, rationale, expected observable outcome, first test, and potential contribution. Omit optional sections that do not help the next decision.

## Completion criterion

The candidate addresses a worthwhile problem, has a concrete proposal and reasonable intuition, and offers a feasible way to learn whether it is worth pursuing. An empirical candidate can advance before its mechanism is fully understood.
